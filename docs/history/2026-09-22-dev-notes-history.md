### Tech #72: three ways to put the won on a chain

- **Cause:** jay pasted a Korean strategy summary arguing that Korean banks should prepare three tracks at once — tokenised deposit, bank-issued stablecoin, third-party stablecoin — and asked for it as an item.
- **Reasoning:** summarising the three tracks would have added nothing, so the item is built on the one question they are three answers to: whose liability is the money. A tokenised deposit leaves the claim where it was and changes only the rail, which is why insurance, interest and the lending relationship survive; a bank-issued coin is a new liability wearing the same name; distributing someone else's coin takes the claim off the balance sheet entirely. That is the generalisation of the keeper from Tech #64 — a payment rail is a balance-sheet position before it is software. Two things the briefing does not say got written in. The second track works **against** the stated goal: reserves backing a bank's own coin come out of the deposit base, so a customer moving from deposit to bank coin has left it just as surely as if they had gone elsewhere, and only the first track is purely defensive. And the two-tier Hangang design maps exactly onto the money hierarchy that already exists — deposit token to deposit, wholesale CBDC to central-bank reserves — which is both the argument for it working and the reason to doubt claims that it changes anything fundamental. A fourth note: permissioned versus public is an access-control choice here, not a technology one, since a KYC whitelist closes a public chain at the account layer, which is the layer a regulated liability cares about.
- **Change:** `docs/topics/pocs-three-tracks-won-onchain.html` at Tech #72 (type Brief, bin `deep`), with a whose-liability table, the two-tier mapping, a Saturday-2am failure test, the Jayverse landing and 16 key expressions. `raw/2026-09-22-korean-bank-deposit-tokens.md` keeps the claims and numbers; **no URL was captured**, so unusually for the raw layer the source cannot be reopened at all, and the file says so.

### The grey badge was five days stale, because keeping it fresh was a manual rule

- **Cause:** jay, with a screenshot of the two rail badges: the red one read 8% · 71/925 and the grey one beside it still read 14% · 61/442. That grey figure is the previous day's closing status, and its tooltip dated it 2026-09-17.
- **Reasoning:** `_progress.js` reads `nav.progress`, a day-by-day log, and shows the latest entry dated before today. The log was maintained by a rule written in the file's own comment — on a new KST day, append the previous day's closing numbers before changing the counters — and that rule had not been followed since the 17th. So the badge was not broken; it was faithfully displaying a log that had stopped being written. A rule a human has to remember on exactly the right day is a rule that will lapse, and this one lapsed for five days while the totals moved from 442 items to 926. The fix is to remove the rule rather than try harder to follow it: upsert **today's** totals on every run instead of trying to catch the moment a day ends. By the end of a day that entry holds the day's closing numbers, and tomorrow it is automatically the latest entry before today. A day with no work simply leaves no entry and the badge falls back to the last day that had any, which its tooltip names.
- **Change:** `roll-done-states.py` upserts today's totals into `nav.progress` on every run, so any item added or status changed keeps the log current. The four missing days were reconstructed from git — the last commit of each KST day that touched `_nav.js` — and appended; jay's four hand-entered days were left exactly as he recorded them, including where they differ from the git-derived figure by an item.
- **Result:** the grey badge now reads 8% · 71/923 dated 2026-09-21 instead of 14% · 61/442 dated 2026-09-17, and it will stay current without anyone remembering to update it. The log also became useful in its own right: 442 items on the 16th, 923 on the 21st, 926 today, with done stuck at 68 through the 19th and 20th.

### Every Eng item now carries pronunciation, and a table header stopped pretending to be data

- **Cause:** jay, 2026-09-23: "for all eng items i want the new words included" — widening the 2026-09-22 rule, which had exempted the 398 items that predated it.
- **Reasoning:** hand-writing 398 pronunciation tables was not the job; choosing words honestly was. The words come from each item's own `## Expressions` table, which is already the curated vocabulary for that conversation, and the pronunciation comes from **CMUdict** — Carnegie Mellon's 126k-entry pronouncing dictionary — converted ARPAbet to IPA. A word CMUdict does not hold is skipped rather than approximated. Two passes were needed: items whose expressions are phrases ("have the nerve to + 동사", "Just because A doesn't mean B") yield no headword short enough to look up, so for those the useful row is the hard word *inside* the phrase, picked by syllable count. Ten items resisted both passes and were written by hand, which is where the best rows are — `dumb /dʌm/` with its silent b, `sine qua non /ˈsaɪni kwɑ ˈnɑn/`, `exude /ɪɡˈzud/` where x is /ɡz/. Two conversion bugs were found by reading the output rather than trusting it: CMUdict marks compounds like `outside` with **two** primary stresses, which printed /ˈaʊˈtsaɪd/, and taking every consonant back to the previous vowel split it as ou-tside because /ts/ is not a legal English onset. Both fixed — the generator now demotes all but the last primary and walks back only through clusters that can legally begin a syllable.
